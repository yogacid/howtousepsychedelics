# HowToUsePsychedelics.com — Voice, Style & Prose Guide

A single reference for anyone editing or writing HTUP content. This guide covers
voice, sentences, openings, closings, paragraphing, and word choice. It does not
cover SEO, structure, schema, or CMS formatting — those live elsewhere.

Run `npm run check:voice` to scan the site for mechanical violations (banned
words and phrases, em-dash density, structural tics). The scanner catches the
countable things only. Everything else on this page is a judgement call.

---

## The Register

HTUP writing sits between two things that most psychedelic content gets wrong in
opposite directions. On one side: clinical hedging and wellness listicles that
are accurate but lifeless. On the other: influencer spirituality that feels alive
but says nothing. HTUP is neither.

The voice is practical prose written with real craft. Clear enough that a nervous
first-timer understands it on one read. Made with enough care that a careful
reader never feels talked down to. The content is grounded in harm reduction and
lived familiarity. The prose is shaped, rhythmic, and earned — not assembled from
a template.

Two instincts run underneath everything. From the practical side: say the useful
thing plainly, and write to one real person. From the literary side: trust the
reader, and earn ideas through observation rather than announcement. These are not
in tension. A sentence can be both direct and well-made. Most of the best HTUP
sentences are.

The test: HTUP answers *how* — how to prepare, how to stay safe, how to make sense
of what happened. It answers that question the way a thoughtful friend would if
that friend also happened to write well.

## The Single Most Important Rule

**Write to one person, and trust them.**

The HTUP reader is usually alone with a real question. Sometimes preparing
quietly. Sometimes trying to make sense of something that already happened. They
are smart, a little wary, and quick to leave the moment they sense boilerplate.
Write to that person — not to a search engine, not to a category, not to a
content strategy.

Trusting them means two things. It means giving them the honest answer, including
the uncertainty. And it means resisting the urge to frame, contextualize, or
justify before you say the thing. State the idea. Let it stand.

❌ "At HTUP, we believe that integration is a multifaceted process encompassing a
wide range of practices."
✅ "Integration is the work you do after the experience. It matters more than most
people expect."

The second version states rather than positions. It respects the reader enough to
skip the throat-clearing.

## What the Voice Sounds Like

**Clear and direct.** Ideas are stated plainly and built on. No unnecessary
complexity, no complexity smuggled in to sound serious.

**Warm but not chatty.** Friendly without filler. The energy is "here's what you
actually need to know" — but earned through usefulness, not announced with
enthusiasm.

**Shaped, not just written.** Sentences have deliberate rhythm. A short sentence
after a longer one is a primary tool. It lands. Use it on purpose, not by accident.

**Confident without being dogmatic.** Harm reduction is the frame, which means
acknowledging uncertainty, individual variation, and risk honestly. Strong
sentences, not hedged ones — but open to complexity where complexity is real.

**Grounded in the concrete.** Examples, scenarios, and real situations over
abstractions. "What does this actually look like?" is the guiding question for
almost every paragraph.

**Humble about what isn't known.** If the research is thin, say so. If practices
vary between traditions, say so. HTUP never claims more certainty than it has, and
never less.

**Personal without performing.** Louis is present in the writing — first person
where it fits. Present, not confessional. The authority comes from having done the
reading and been through it, not from a brand name.

## What the Voice Does Not Sound Like

- Academic or clinical ("contraindication profiles," "adverse event frequency,"
  "therapeutic modality")
- Influencer psychedelia ("this changed my life," "pure magic," "the mushrooms
  spoke to me," "high vibration")
- Corporate wellness ("unlock your potential," "5 life-changing benefits of…")
- Overcautious legal hedging that treats every sentence as a liability ("we cannot
  recommend or endorse…")
- A blunt harm-reduction pamphlet — accurate but cold, with no sense of the human
  on the other side
- Breathless concept-stacking that never lands anywhere
- **A framework essay.** Depth is welcome, but the deep philosophical *why* belongs
  to Psygaia. When a piece opens onto that question, gesture toward it or link out
  — don't try to answer it here.

## Sentences & Rhythm

**Vary sentence length deliberately.** One idea per sentence is the default, and it
keeps the writing readable — but a longer, developed sentence followed by a short
one is how you create emphasis and breathing room. Build the rhythm on purpose.

**Keep sentences accessible even when they're long.** Length is fine; density is
not. Never stack more than three clauses. If a sentence makes the reader work to
hold it together, split it.

**Read everything aloud.** If you trip over it, rewrite it. If it sounds like a
brochure, rewrite it.

**Avoid jargon** unless you define it in the same breath or the reader clearly
already knows it.

**Punctuation.** Avoid semicolons in blog prose — they interrupt rhythm and usually
mean a sentence wants to be two. Em-dashes: no more than one per 400 words, and
never as a default connector. A pair of em-dashes bracketing a clause counts as
one. Reach for a comma or a period first.

## Openings

Open on something concrete: a real situation, a common mistake, a question someone
actually types into a search bar, or the gap between what people expect and what
actually happens. Let the idea emerge from the observation. The reader should feel
understood within the first three sentences — *yes, this is exactly what I was
looking for.*

✅ "Most people spend weeks researching their substance and ten minutes thinking
about their setting. It's almost always the setting that matters more."
✅ "Bicycle Day and Earth Day fall three days apart on the calendar. A small
coincidence, or not."
❌ "In recent years, psychedelics have garnered increasing attention as tools for
healing and transformation…"

**Two hard rules.** Never open with a rhetorical question that could open any
article ("Have you ever wondered…"). If you open with a question at all, it must be
one you genuinely can't answer quickly, and it should haunt the rest of the piece.
And earn the first sentence: if you can cut it and the piece still stands, cut it.

## Closings

End with something the reader can do, think, or come back to. Not a generic gesture
toward encouragement, gratitude, or the mission.

The strongest closings do one of two things: they reframe something from the
opening, or they arrive somewhere slightly unexpected through the logic of the
piece itself. If the ending doesn't surprise even a little, it hasn't landed.

A short final line after a longer closing paragraph works well — it names what the
reader now has. "Now you have a framework. Use it before the experience, not
during."

**What to avoid:** bow-wrapping ("remember, you're supported on this journey"),
announcing importance ("this is important work"), and "rooted in" as a closing
gesture. It's become filler.

## Paragraphing, Lists & Structure

Keep paragraphs short to medium — two to four sentences through most of a piece.
Longer paragraphs, up to six sentences, are fine when you're genuinely building an
argument or explaining a mechanism. Break the moment a new idea begins.

**On lists.** This is where HTUP parts ways from pure essay writing, and it does so
deliberately. Lists are allowed here — but only when the content is genuinely
list-shaped: preparation steps, checklist items, warning signs, a sequence someone
will follow in order. A checklist someone reads before a session is doing real work.

The rule that keeps this honest: never convert prose into bullets to create visual
rhythm or to avoid writing the connective tissue. If you're tempted to make a list
of *ideas* — reasons, concepts, arguments — that temptation means you haven't done
the work of turning them into prose. Do the work. And whenever a list does appear,
surround it with prose that explains why each item matters, not just what it is. A
bare list with no context is a pamphlet, not an HTUP article.

## Word Choice

### Use Sparingly

These words belong to the space and readers recognize them. They lose meaning as
default filler. Earn them.

- **"Journey"** — fine for a genuinely extended process; avoid when a more specific
  word would do the work.
- **"Transformative"** — use when something actually warrants the weight; never as a
  default intensifier.
- **"Navigate"** — good for genuinely uncertain terrain; avoid when "handle,"
  "approach," or "work through" is more precise.
- **"Integration"** — central to the vocabulary, and easy to hollow out. Always be
  specific: integration of what, into what, how.
- **"Sacred"** — only with earned specificity and full intention.
- **"Ancient wisdom"** — only when you name the actual tradition or teaching.

### Vocabulary That Belongs

**Practical spine:** preparation, readiness, set and setting, dose, sensitivity,
individual variation. Grounded, clear-headed, present. Difficult experience,
challenging moment, hard passage. Sitter, guide, trusted person. Before, during,
and after. Risk, informed decision, awareness. What to expect, what actually
happens, what helps.

**Relational register, used lightly** and only when the content opens onto meaning:
reconnection, relational, interdependence, the more-than-human world, belonging,
attunement. This vocabulary carries real weight — reach for it when a piece
genuinely touches the deeper layer, and hand the reader to Psygaia when they want
to go further. Don't sprinkle it for flavor.

### Banned Language

**Words — never use**

delve, tapestry, leverage, streamline, cutting-edge, multifaceted, holistic,
paradigm, game-changer / game-changing, groundbreaking, unlock, unleash, empower,
foster, harness, spearhead, embark, high vibration, raising consciousness.

The test for this list is whether the word is doing work. These are words that
arrive as filler — they signal effort without adding meaning, and most of them
are tells for text that was assembled rather than written.

**Words that are only sometimes a problem**

- **evidence-based** — belongs to the clinical framing HTUP sits apart from.
- **landmark** — fine for a study that genuinely was one, filler otherwise.

These are flagged for a human to judge rather than banned outright.

**Deliberately not banned.** Ordinary English with a legitimate technical sense
here was removed from this list in August 2026, after a site-wide sweep showed it
firing almost entirely on correct usage: *robust* ("robust evidence"), *elevate*
("elevated mood," "elevates the risk"), *synergy* (a pharmacological category in
the interaction tables), *therapeutic*, *landscape* ("the legal landscape"), and
*holding space* (the actual subject of `/facilitation/`). A word with a real
technical meaning in this domain is not filler, and banning it costs more than it
saves. Prefer the plainer word where one exists; that is a judgement, not a rule.

**Phrases — never use**

- "It's worth noting that…" / "It's important to remember that…"
- "In today's [anything]…" / "In the world of…"
- "At HTUP, we believe…"
- "As we navigate these uncertain times…"
- "Let's dive in" / "Let's get into it"
- "The good news is…" / "Here's the thing:"
- "At its core…"
- "The universe is telling us…"

**Structural patterns — never use**

- Rhetorical-question openers ("Have you ever wondered…")
- **"Not X. Y." / "It's not X, it's Y."** — an overused tic; use once in a great
  while, never as a reflex
- Three-part parallel flourishes used for rhythm rather than meaning
- Generic bow-wrapping closings
- Any self-introduction opener ("Hi, I'm Louis, and today I want to talk about…")

**Framing — never use**

- Hedging that paralyzes the article's usefulness ("we can't recommend…")
- Guru tone ("here's what you NEED to know")
- Superlatives dressed as fact ("the single most important thing you can do")
- Any framing that positions HTUP as a brand making claims rather than a voice
  making observations

## The Blend in One Example

The same idea, written three ways:

❌ **Clinical:** "Individuals should ensure adequate environmental optimization
prior to substance administration, as setting variables demonstrably influence
experiential outcomes."

❌ **Influencer:** "Your space holds so much energy — trust it, honor it, and let
the medicine meet you exactly where you are. ✨"

✅ **HTUP:** "Your setting shapes the experience more than the dose does. That
sounds like an exaggeration until the first time a familiar room turns strange on
you. Spend real time on where you'll be, and who's there."

The third version is plain enough to trust and made with enough care to keep
reading. That's the whole target.

**Companion register: Psygaia** — for the deeper "why." When an HTUP piece opens
onto meaning, ecology, or the philosophical question underneath the practical one,
gesture toward it and cross-link. Keep each voice doing what it does best.
